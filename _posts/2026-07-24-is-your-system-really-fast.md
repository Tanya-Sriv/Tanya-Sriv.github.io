<h3>Why Burst Performance and Sustained Performance Tell Different Stories</h3><p>Your benchmark says the system is fast.</p><p>Great.</p><p>But will it still be fast an hour later?</p><p>What happens when traffic suddenly doubles?</p><p>What if a GPU reaches its thermal limit?</p><p>What if queues begin growing slightly faster than they can drain?</p><p>What if a small resource leak remains invisible during a short test but becomes a serious problem after several hours?</p><p>These questions reveal a common gap in performance testing.</p><p>Most benchmarks answer:</p><blockquote><strong><em>How fast did the system run?</em></strong></blockquote><p>A more useful evaluation also asks:</p><blockquote><strong><em>How quickly did the workload arrive?</em></strong></blockquote><blockquote><strong><em>How long did the system sustain it?</em></strong></blockquote><blockquote><strong><em>What changed as the test continued?</em></strong></blockquote><p>A system can handle a short spike and still degrade during long-running execution.</p><p>Another system may remain stable for hours under predictable load but struggle when demand rises suddenly.</p><p>These are different capabilities.</p><p>Testing one does not prove the other.</p><h3>The 2-Minute Version</h3><p>Performance testing is often reduced to a few numbers:</p><ul><li>latency</li><li>throughput</li><li>utilization</li><li>execution time</li><li>maximum concurrency</li></ul><p>These numbers matter, but they can hide how the system behaves under different kinds of pressure.</p><p>A short, intense workload can reveal:</p><ul><li>queue saturation</li><li>sudden latency spikes</li><li>scheduler delays</li><li>dropped requests</li><li>memory-bandwidth contention</li><li>slow recovery after overload</li></ul><p>A long-running workload can reveal:</p><ul><li>thermal throttling</li><li>memory leaks</li><li>fragmentation</li><li>growing backlogs</li><li>performance drift</li><li>retry accumulation</li><li>resource starvation</li></ul><p>The key distinction is simple:</p><p><strong>Burst performance shows how a system reacts to sudden demand.</strong></p><p><strong>Sustained performance shows how well it holds up over time.</strong></p><p>A system should not be considered fast simply because it performs well during a short benchmark.</p><p>It should also remain stable, predictable, and efficient when the workload continues.</p><h3>Performance Is More Than a Peak Number</h3><p>Performance reports often highlight impressive results:</p><ul><li>peak throughput</li><li>average latency</li><li>requests per second</li><li>benchmark completion time</li><li>GPU utilization</li><li>maximum supported load</li></ul><p>These metrics are useful, but they do not always explain how the system behaved during the test.</p><p>Imagine two systems completing the same amount of work.</p><p>One processes it during a sudden 30-second spike.</p><p>The other processes it continuously over several hours.</p><p>The total work may be similar, but the systems were stressed in very different ways.</p><p>The first scenario tests immediate response capacity.</p><p>The second tests stability, thermal behavior, resource management, and performance retention.</p><p>A single average or peak value can hide that difference.</p><h3>What Is Burst Performance?</h3><p>Burst performance describes how a system reacts when demand rises sharply for a short period.</p><p>Examples include:</p><ul><li>an API receiving a sudden traffic spike</li><li>an inference service receiving many requests at once</li><li>a GPU test farm receiving thousands of new jobs</li><li>a CI/CD platform processing a wave of commits</li><li>a game entering a graphically intensive scene</li><li>multiple workloads competing for the same accelerator</li><li>a database receiving a sudden increase in transactions</li></ul><p>A burst test is not only about high load.</p><p>It is also about how quickly the load appears.</p><p>A system may handle the final load level eventually but still respond poorly during the transition.</p><p>That short transition can expose serious problems.</p><h3>What Burst Testing Can Reveal</h3><h3>Queue saturation</h3><p>When work arrives faster than it can be processed, queues grow quickly.</p><p>The system may still appear active, and throughput may remain acceptable for a while.</p><p>But waiting time increases.</p><p>Eventually, users experience slower responses, timeouts, or dropped work.</p><h3>Tail-latency spikes</h3><p>Average latency can look healthy while a smaller percentage of requests experience severe delays.</p><p>This matters in systems where one operation depends on several services, workers, or processing stages.</p><p>One slow component can delay the entire result.</p><p>That is why p95 and p99 latency often reveal more than the average alone.</p><h3>Scheduler reaction delays</h3><p>A scheduler may perform well under steady load but react too slowly when demand changes suddenly.</p><p>During that period:</p><ul><li>some workers may become overloaded</li><li>other capable workers may remain idle</li><li>short jobs may wait behind long jobs</li><li>high-priority work may be delayed</li><li>queues may become uneven</li></ul><p>The issue may not be a lack of capacity.</p><p>It may be that available capacity is not assigned quickly or intelligently enough.</p><h3>Shared-resource contention</h3><p>Sudden bursts can create competition for:</p><ul><li>memory bandwidth</li><li>storage</li><li>network links</li><li>database connections</li><li>locks</li><li>caches</li><li>driver submission paths</li><li>synchronization points</li></ul><p>These bottlenecks may last only a short time and disappear inside an average calculated over the entire test.</p><h3>Admission-control failures</h3><p>A system should not always accept unlimited incoming work.</p><p>Sometimes it must:</p><ul><li>reject requests</li><li>delay lower-priority jobs</li><li>apply backpressure</li><li>reduce optional work</li><li>batch similar tasks</li><li>protect critical operations</li></ul><p>Without these controls, one spike can degrade the entire platform.</p><h3>Slow recovery</h3><p>A system may survive the spike but remain unhealthy afterward.</p><p>Queues may take time to drain.</p><p>Retries may continue.</p><p>Latency may remain high.</p><p>Resources may not be released immediately.</p><p>That is why the recovery period should be measured as part of the burst test.</p><h3>What Is Sustained Performance?</h3><p>Sustained performance describes how well a system maintains acceptable behavior over a longer period.</p><p>The workload does not always need to be extreme.</p><p>Even a normal production workload can reveal problems when it continues long enough.</p><p>Sustained testing asks:</p><blockquote><strong><em>What changes after the system has been running for a while?</em></strong></blockquote><p>Some issues appear immediately.</p><p>Others develop gradually.</p><h3>What Sustained Testing Can Reveal</h3><h3>Memory and resource leaks</h3><p>A small leak may not matter during a ten-minute benchmark.</p><p>Over several hours or millions of operations, it can become significant.</p><p>The buildup may involve:</p><ul><li>system memory</li><li>GPU memory</li><li>file handles</li><li>database sessions</li><li>threads</li><li>temporary files</li><li>event objects</li><li>logging buffers</li><li>containers</li><li>background processes</li></ul><p>The problem is often not the initial amount.</p><p>It is the direction of growth.</p><h3>Memory fragmentation</h3><p>A system may report available memory and still fail to allocate what it needs.</p><p>Repeated allocation and release of differently sized blocks can make usable memory harder to obtain.</p><p>This is especially relevant to:</p><ul><li>GPU workloads</li><li>inference services</li><li>databases</li><li>embedded systems</li><li>multimedia pipelines</li><li>long-running simulations</li></ul><h3>Thermal throttling</h3><p>A processor or GPU may perform extremely well at the beginning of a test.</p><p>The hardware is still cool, and higher clock speeds may be temporarily available.</p><p>As temperature increases, the system may reduce frequency to remain within thermal or power limits.</p><p>This means the first few minutes may show burst capability, not sustainable capability.</p><p>A practical test should compare:</p><ul><li>initial performance</li><li>performance after warm-up</li><li>performance after temperature stabilizes</li><li>clock frequency over time</li><li>temperature over time</li><li>power behavior</li></ul><h3>Queue accumulation</h3><p>A system can appear healthy while slowly falling behind.</p><p>Suppose incoming work is only slightly higher than the rate the system can sustain.</p><p>The difference may be too small to notice during a short test.</p><p>Over time, however:</p><ul><li>queue depth grows</li><li>waiting time increases</li><li>timeouts become more likely</li><li>recovery takes longer</li><li>new work begins competing with old backlog</li></ul><p>The system is still running, but it is not operating sustainably.</p><h3>Performance drift</h3><p>Not every performance problem appears as a sudden failure.</p><p>The system may slowly become less efficient because of:</p><ul><li>cache changes</li><li>garbage collection</li><li>log growth</li><li>background maintenance</li><li>storage compaction</li><li>retry traffic</li><li>scheduler imbalance</li><li>thermal effects</li><li>increasing contention</li><li>allocator behavior</li></ul><p>A single average across the full test can hide this trend.</p><p>The beginning, middle, and end of the run should be compared separately.</p><h3>Rare failures</h3><p>Some failures appear only after a large number of operations.</p><p>Examples include:</p><ul><li>race conditions</li><li>intermittent timeouts</li><li>deadlocks</li><li>synchronization issues</li><li>resets</li><li>corruption</li><li>failed recovery paths</li></ul><p>Long-running tests provide reliability information that short benchmarks cannot.</p><h3>Burst and Sustained Performance Are Different Capabilities</h3><p>Burst performance answers:</p><blockquote><strong><em>How well does the system react to sudden pressure?</em></strong></blockquote><p>Sustained performance answers:</p><blockquote><strong><em>How well does it maintain acceptable behavior over time?</em></strong></blockquote><p>Both matter.</p><p>Burst performance is important for:</p><ul><li>sudden traffic changes</li><li>interactive applications</li><li>short inference requests</li><li>user-interface responsiveness</li><li>release events</li><li>graphics transitions</li><li>event-driven workloads</li></ul><p>Sustained performance is important for:</p><ul><li>long-running inference</li><li>model training</li><li>gaming sessions</li><li>rendering</li><li>regression execution</li><li>data processing</li><li>simulations</li><li>embedded applications</li></ul><p>A strong result in one category does not guarantee a strong result in the other.</p><h3>Why Average Metrics Hide the Story</h3><p>Imagine a service running for one hour.</p><p>For the first 50 minutes, latency remains low.</p><p>During the final ten minutes, it increases sharply because queues are growing, memory pressure is rising, or the system is throttling.</p><p>The overall average may still appear acceptable.</p><p>But users during the final interval experience a degraded system.</p><p>Throughput can be misleading in the same way.</p><p>A single reported result may hide a pattern such as:</p><ul><li>very high throughput at startup</li><li>lower performance after warm-up</li><li>another reduction after thermal stabilization</li><li>periodic stalls</li><li>temporary recovery when queues drain</li></ul><p>The average removes the timeline.</p><p>But the timeline often contains the most useful information.</p><h3>A Simple Performance-Test Matrix</h3><p>A practical testing strategy should cover more than one workload shape.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*10Jrg4uuZWoQ_pd0hU33Hw.png" /></figure><p>Each test answers a different question.</p><p>One benchmark should not be treated as proof for all of them.</p><h3>Metrics Engineers Should Track</h3><h3>Latency</h3><p>Track:</p><ul><li>median latency</li><li>p95 latency</li><li>p99 latency</li><li>worst observed latency</li><li>latency by time window</li></ul><p>This helps separate typical behavior from slow outliers.</p><h3>Throughput</h3><p>Track:</p><ul><li>peak throughput</li><li>stabilized throughput</li><li>lowest rolling-window throughput</li><li>successfully completed work</li><li>failed work</li><li>retried work</li><li>duplicate work</li></ul><p>Peak throughput should be labeled clearly.</p><p>It should not automatically be treated as sustained capacity.</p><h3>Queue behavior</h3><p>Track:</p><ul><li>queue depth</li><li>age of the oldest queued item</li><li>scheduling delay</li><li>time spent waiting</li><li>queue-drain time</li><li>whether the queue keeps growing under steady input</li></ul><p>A queue that rises continuously is often a stronger warning than a temporary utilization spike.</p><h3>Resource behavior</h3><p>Depending on the system, monitor:</p><ul><li>CPU utilization</li><li>GPU utilization</li><li>memory usage</li><li>memory-bandwidth pressure</li><li>clock frequency</li><li>temperature</li><li>power</li><li>storage activity</li><li>network activity</li><li>cache behavior</li><li>allocation failures</li></ul><p>High utilization does not always mean high efficiency.</p><p>A system can remain busy while spending significant time on retries, waiting, stalled execution, or work that does not contribute to the final result.</p><h3>Reliability</h3><p>Track:</p><ul><li>error rate</li><li>timeout rate</li><li>retry rate</li><li>crashes</li><li>resets</li><li>correctness failures</li><li>lost work</li><li>allocation failures</li><li>recovery failures</li></ul><p>Performance is not meaningful if it is achieved by dropping or repeating work.</p><h3>Recovery</h3><p>Track:</p><ul><li>time to return to normal latency</li><li>time to drain queues</li><li>time to release resources</li><li>number of errors after stress</li><li>whether manual intervention is required</li></ul><p>A resilient system should return to a healthy state predictably.</p><h3>A Practical Testing Sequence</h3><p>Not every test needs to run on every commit.</p><p>A layered strategy is usually more practical.</p><h3>1. Run a short baseline test</h3><p>Use a repeatable workload under normal conditions.</p><p>This helps establish expected latency, throughput, and resource usage.</p><h3>2. Add a burst test</h3><p>Increase demand quickly for a short period.</p><p>Observe queue growth, tail latency, scheduling behavior, and dropped work.</p><h3>3. Measure recovery</h3><p>Reduce the load and continue monitoring.</p><p>Check how long queues, latency, errors, and resource usage take to return to normal.</p><h3>4. Run a sustained test</h3><p>Use a realistic workload for a longer period.</p><p>Look for leaks, thermal effects, performance drift, and growing backlog.</p><h3>5. Run sustained high-load tests selectively</h3><p>Use these before releases or after major changes involving:</p><ul><li>scheduling</li><li>memory management</li><li>concurrency</li><li>power management</li><li>thermal behavior</li><li>resource cleanup</li><li>queue handling</li><li>recovery logic</li></ul><h3>6. Test failure behavior</h3><p>Push beyond intended capacity occasionally.</p><p>Confirm that the system:</p><ul><li>rejects work predictably</li><li>preserves critical services</li><li>avoids uncontrolled failure</li><li>recovers without manual cleanup</li></ul><h3>Common Testing Mistakes</h3><h3>Testing only from a cold state</h3><p>Fresh processes, empty queues, unused memory, and cool hardware create unusually favorable conditions.</p><p>Include warm-up and steady-state behavior.</p><h3>Treating peak performance as sustained performance</h3><p>A temporary boost rate is not the same as a rate the system can maintain.</p><p>Label the result accurately.</p><h3>Reporting only averages</h3><p>Include percentiles, time-series views, and worst intervals.</p><h3>Ending the test when the workload ends</h3><p>Continue observing the system during recovery.</p><h3>Using only constant workloads</h3><p>Real systems experience:</p><ul><li>bursts</li><li>idle periods</li><li>mixed job sizes</li><li>priority changes</li><li>retries</li><li>resource competition</li></ul><p>Constant load is useful, but incomplete.</p><h3>Ignoring wasted work</h3><p>Retries, duplicate execution, invalid outputs, and abandoned jobs can make utilization look high without producing equivalent value.</p><h3>Assuming a short pass proves long-term stability</h3><p>A system that runs correctly for ten minutes has demonstrated ten minutes of behavior.</p><p>It has not demonstrated ten hours.</p><h3>Why This Matters for GPU and Accelerator Systems</h3><p>The distinction between burst and sustained performance is especially important for GPUs and accelerators.</p><p>A short GPU test may reveal:</p><ul><li>launch overhead</li><li>scheduling delay</li><li>memory-bandwidth contention</li><li>synchronization stalls</li><li>sudden power spikes</li><li>submission bottlenecks</li><li>immediate out-of-memory failures</li></ul><p>A long-running GPU test may reveal:</p><ul><li>thermal throttling</li><li>clock-frequency reduction</li><li>memory leakage</li><li>allocator fragmentation</li><li>cooling limits</li><li>repeated resets</li><li>queue imbalance</li><li>reduced performance per watt</li></ul><p>GPU utilization alone does not explain these effects.</p><p>A GPU may show high activity while:</p><ul><li>waiting on memory</li><li>processing retries</li><li>running at a reduced frequency</li><li>executing work that will be discarded</li><li>blocking another stage</li><li>contributing little to final completed work</li></ul><p>A more useful performance view combines:</p><ul><li>utilization</li><li>completed work</li><li>latency</li><li>queue behavior</li><li>frequency</li><li>temperature</li><li>power</li><li>errors</li><li>stability over time</li><li>recovery behavior</li></ul><h3>Final Thoughts</h3><p>Performance is not simply the highest number reached during a benchmark.</p><p>It is the ability to produce correct and useful work:</p><ul><li>when demand arrives suddenly</li><li>when the workload continues</li><li>when queues begin to build</li><li>when the hardware warms up</li><li>when rare failures have time to appear</li><li>and when the system must recover afterward</li></ul><p>Some systems fail because demand arrives too quickly.</p><p>Others fail because it lasts too long.</p><p>Many problems remain hidden because we test only one of those conditions.</p><p>The better question is not simply:</p><blockquote><strong><em>How fast is the system?</em></strong></blockquote><p>It is:</p><blockquote><strong><em>How fast is it, under what kind of pressure, and for how long?</em></strong></blockquote><p>Because in real systems:</p><blockquote><strong><em>Time is part of performance.</em></strong></blockquote><h3>Disclaimer</h3><p>This article presents
